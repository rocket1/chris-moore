This is Radio Caroline on 199, your all-day music station.

The first words broadcast from a ship anchored off the Essex coast at noon on Easter Saturday, 28 March 1964 — and the moment British radio stopped belonging to the BBC alone.

Chris Moore

16 April 1940 – 2 January 2021

He was six foot six with a bass voice that filled a room, and for a few years in the middle of the 1960s he was at the centre of the most disruptive thing to happen to British broadcasting in a generation. Chris Moore helped start Radio Caroline, found the ship it sailed on, hired the disc jockeys who became household names, and then quietly stepped away from all of it.

He spent the next fifty years painting, taking photographs, living in rooms he cheerfully described as basic, and swimming off a Welsh cliff every summer. This page is for anyone who knew him, worked with him, or grew up with a transistor radio under the bedclothes tuned to 199 metres.

Share a memory of Chris

America, then Knightsbridge

1940

An American childhood, mostly unrecorded

Chris was born on 16 April 1940 and brought to England at the age of eight by his American mother. She married an Englishman, and the family lived in a large Knightsbridge flat; Chris was sent off to boarding school. Friends later recalled him dropping tantalising hints about his parents' circle — names from the world of 1950s diplomacy and espionage — without ever filling in much detail. Technically he remained an American all his life.

Radio Caroline's 1964 press office said he was born in Washington DC, and that is the version most obituaries carried. Later research by Mervyn Hagger, cited by the Pirate Radio Hall of Fame, points to California instead.

Jermyn Street, St James's

Early 60s

The Crazy Elephant

Before radio there was the Merchant Navy, where he worked as a steward — useful experience, as it turned out — and a spell in advertising. Then he started spinning records at the Crazy Elephant, a club on Jermyn Street part-owned by the film producer Benny Fisz and permanently full of actors, models and people hoping to be either. Chris was a fixture of that scene. He was reportedly offered the role of Bond himself, in the gap between Sean Connery and George Lazenby.

Some accounts put the Crazy Elephant on Curzon Street, perhaps confusing it with the White Elephant Club there. TIME, in March 1964, placed it in Jermyn Street: “come-one-come-all… where the beat is blue, the mood frenetic, and the Shake is the thing.”

Three miles off Felixstowe

1964

Three men, a ship and £250,000

Chris knew Ronan O'Rahilly, who wanted to put a radio station on a boat. It was Chris who introduced him to Ian Ross, his flatmate in Chelsea, whose father became one of the station's principal backers and helped raise the quarter of a million pounds the venture needed. And it was Chris who organised the purchase of the ship itself — a Danish passenger ferry, the MV Fredericia, refitted with a transmitter and a tall mast and re-anchored in international water off Felixstowe.

At noon on Easter Saturday, 28 March 1964, his was the first voice on British offshore radio. The first record he played was the Rolling Stones' Not Fade Away, dedicated to Ronan.

Accounts of that first record differ — some tellings name a different Stones single, others a Beatles one. Not Fade Away, dedicated to O'Rahilly, is the version given by the Pirate Radio Hall of Fame. If you were listening that afternoon, we would very much like to hear from you.

1964–70

Programme director

He didn't stay at sea for long. The first full programme went to Simon Dee, and Chris moved ashore to become Caroline's first programme director. From there he hired the presenters — several of whom the BBC would later poach for the brand-new Radio 1, a station created largely to answer the pirates. He also spent years on the unglamorous work: keeping a station afloat, literally and financially, and keeping it outside territorial waters and beyond the reach of prosecution.

By 1967 Caroline had millions of listeners. In 1991 Chris told the story properly, at length, for the BBC documentary A Pirate's Tale. He is a member of the Pirate Radio Hall of Fame.

Oxfordshire

Mid 70s

Barcote Manor

Worn down by the endless work of keeping Caroline going, Chris was visiting a girlfriend in rural Oxfordshire when he came across Barcote Manor: a gothic pile run as something between a household and a commune. He took a tiny room there and stayed for twenty years, painting and photographing prolifically and sending himself up while he did it. He shot pictures for the Museum of Modern Art in Oxford. When the owner sold up in the mid-nineties, he moved Chris — by then a close friend — into a small cottage nearby.

Valle Gran Rey, the Canary Islands

Meanwhile

A cave on La Gomera

Years before it became a destination, Chris arrived at La Gomera in the Canaries by private boat and spent several months living in a cave near the Valle Gran Rey — the first outsider to do so, before the valley turned into a hippy retreat spoken of alongside Goa and Ibiza. He went back often during the Barcote years and took a stream of friends with him.

The Gower, South Wales

25 summers

Caswell Bay

He bought a rough wooden bungalow on the hillside above Caswell Bay on the Gower, and for the next quarter-century he spent his summers there: riding down each spring on an ex-police BMW motorbike, swimming, climbing back up the cliff, and keeping the rain out with inspired and precarious carpentry. Basic accommodation was his natural habitat — the rougher the better — and he occupied it as though it were a palace, holding court for whoever turned up.

Oxfordshire

2021

The last of it

The trips to Wales stopped a couple of years before the end, as the floorboards gave out and the motorbike turned unreliable. Around the same time two women, Becka and Nay, moved in next door and became close friends, keeping an eye on him as he grew more sedentary and his health quietly declined. When Covid arrived they did his shopping. When his throat began troubling him in October 2020 they arranged his medical care and drove him to his appointments.

He was diagnosed with oesophageal cancer and died peacefully on 2 January 2021, aged eighty. He was cremated on the 22nd.

What it meant

Chris helped put the ship in the water and spoke the first words from it. This is what the people who worked on it, and the people who listened, have said it did.

If a ship was parked off the coast, three miles out, the whole country would tune in and turn on.

Ronan O'Rahilly, Radio Caroline's founder, on the idea Chris turned into a ship — quoted in The Irish Post

Pirate radio, and in particular Radio Caroline, was a really exciting part of all our lives in those days and summed up the spirit of the times culturally and musically.

Paul McCartney — quoted in Flashes & Flames, 2024

Back in the '60s, kids in America had a wealth of radio stations to listen to. Here in the U.K., we had one, the BBC, and they hadn't discovered The Beatles. We needed Radio Caroline, from a ship three miles off the coast, to hear that pop music that we craved for.

Ray Clark, broadcaster and Caroline historian — ABC News, 2024

After a couple of weeks we had more people listening than all of the BBC networks combined and that got up their nose somewhat because they were convinced there was no demand for this kind of music

Peter Moore, Radio Caroline station manager — ABC News (Australia), 2017

I would say today it was the equivalent of taking away Facebook, Twitter, all the social media. It was that important to us. It linked us all. It was the fabric of our lives.

Steve Anthony, a sixteen-year-old listener when the pirates were outlawed in August 1967 — ABC News (Australia), 2017

I saw this little pirate ship giving the government so much trouble and I thought 'wow, that's great'.

Tony Blackburn, Caroline DJ and later the first voice on BBC Radio 1 — East Anglian Daily Times, 2024

I think it lit a spark. It was just so special and so different and worth fighting for.

Ray Clark — ABC News (Australia), 2017
